Our role: processor or controller

Which hat we wear depends on which data you mean, and the distinction matters for your rights.

Data you put into Attaché — we are the processor

The accounts, contacts, deals, notes, tasks and files in your workspace belong to you. You decide what goes in, why it is there, and how long it stays. We process it on your instructions in order to provide the service. Under the GDPR and similar laws, you are the controller and we are the processor, and the people whose details you store are the data subjects.

If one of your contacts asks us directly what we hold about them, we will not answer for you. We will refer them to you and help you respond.

Your own account and billing data — we are the controller

The name, work email and password of the people on your account, your subscription and payment records, and our server and usage logs are data we determine the purposes for. For that narrow set, we are the controller.

Information we collect

Information you give us

  • Account information — the name, work email address and password of each person you give a seat to, and the organization name.
  • Workspace content — everything you or your team enter or import: accounts, contacts, deals, quotes, notes, tasks, expenses, attachments.
  • Support correspondence — what you write to us when you ask for help.

Information that arrives because you connected something

  • Calendar entries, and a passing digest of recent mail, if and only if a user on your account connects a mailbox. The mail digest is read to answer a question and is not stored. Section 4 covers this in full.
  • Public comments on a YouTube channel somebody chose to watch, if and only if a user on your account connects one. The commenter’s public display name and channel id, and the text of the comment, are cached for no longer than 30 days. Section 5 covers this in full.

Information we generate

  • Authentication records — every sign-in, multi-factor challenge, pass, failure, enrollment and sign-out is appended to an audit log.
  • Audit trail — every change to your data is recorded with who made it and when.
  • AI usage metering — for each AI action, the kind of job, the model tier and the token count, so that your usage dashboard can show you what the AI layer did and what it cost.
  • Technical logs — ordinary server logs: request paths, timestamps, IP address, error traces.

We do not buy personal data about you from data brokers to enrich your account, and we do not build advertising profiles.

Connected mailboxes

Connecting a mailbox is optional, it is off until a user turns it on, and it is the part of Attaché that touches the most sensitive information. So here is exactly what happens.

What we ask for

When you connect a Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace mailbox, you are shown your provider's own consent screen listing the permissions we request. They are:

That is the whole list. On the direct Microsoft and Google connections we do not request access to your calendar, your contacts, your files or your drive, and we cannot read them. The read permission is deliberately the narrow, read-only one: we cannot modify or delete anything in your mailbox.

We never send mail on your behalf on our own initiative. The send permission is used only when a person on your account composes and sends a message in Attaché. We do not send marketing from your address, and we do not auto-reply.

What we store: nothing out of the mailbox itself

We do not store your messages. There is no mail ingest and no copy of your inbox on our side. When somebody on your account asks their agent about recent mail, we read a digest from your provider at that moment — sender, subject, date and your provider's own one-line snippet, the same fields your own inbox list shows you — and use it to answer the question. Message bodies and attachments are never fetched from your provider, which is what makes it impossible for them to be stored, logged or sent to a model. Attachments you deliberately save onto a record are stored, because you put them there. Where calendar sync is in use, we store an event's subject, time, participants and conferencing link — not its full description.

An earlier version of this statement said we stored subjects and full message bodies. We did, and we stopped: the connector that took mail in was removed from the product on 09-26-2026 and the stored copies went with it. This paragraph is the correction, left visible rather than quietly swapped.

Turning it off

You can disconnect a mailbox in Attaché at any time, and you can independently revoke our access from your Microsoft or Google account settings without involving us. Revoking stops all further access immediately — and because nothing out of the mailbox was stored, there is no mail of yours left on our side to delete afterwards.

Connected YouTube channels

Watching a YouTube channel is optional, it is off until somebody on your account connects one, and most of what it reads was written by other people — the fans who left the comments. So, as with mail above, here is exactly what happens.

What we ask for, and what we read

Nothing, and there is no consent screen — because we do not sign in as you and we hold no permission on your channel. Attaché reads the public comments on a channel you name, through YouTube’s public Data API, using a key of our own. These are the same comments any visitor to the video can read. You can name up to three channels, and they do not have to be yours.

Reading public data is the only thing that key can do. It cannot see your YouTube analytics, your private or unlisted videos, your subscriber list, your messages, or anything a signed-out visitor cannot already see.

That feature uses YouTube API Services. Using it means the YouTube Terms of Service apply to you, and Google’s Privacy Policy governs what Google does with the request we make. Because the key is ours rather than a sign-in as you, there is no Google permission of yours to revoke — the way to end it is to stop watching the channel, described at the end of this section.

What we store

For each comment we keep the commenter’s public display name and channel id, the text of the comment, which video it was on, when it was posted, its like count, and the moment we fetched it. Beside that we keep what our AI layer made of it — which kind of comment it is, and a drafted reply — and whether somebody on your account has marked it dealt with. The comment text is sent to Google Gemini to produce those two things, under the same terms described in section 7.

Thirty days, and whose rule it is

Cached comments are deleted 30 days after we fetched them, and that limit is YouTube’s rather than ours. The YouTube API Services Developer Policies allow a developer to hold public data of this kind for no longer than 30 calendar days before deleting or refreshing it. We honour it by deleting: a daily job removes the whole row — display name, channel id, comment text, our classification and the drafted reply all go at the same moment — and writes down how many it removed.

Two details make that a real limit rather than a stated one. Nothing past 30 days is shown even before the job runs: every screen and every read is bounded by the same cutoff the deletion uses, so an expired comment is unreadable from the moment it expires, not from the moment the job next wakes up. And if that job has not run for two days, the module stops reading new comments altogether rather than collecting rows it cannot promise to sweep.

Nothing is ever posted

Attaché cannot post to YouTube, and that is a fact about the credential rather than a policy we are keeping. The reply our AI layer writes is a draft on a screen, for a person to read, change and paste into YouTube themselves. Posting a comment requires signing in as the channel owner, which we never do and never ask you for. There is no setting that turns posting on, because there is nothing to turn on.

When a commenter becomes a contact

A commenter is not a contact. They become one only when a person on your account presses the button that makes them one, one at a time — nothing in here bulk-imports an audience into your contact book. A contact made that way is your own workspace content and outlives the 30 days, because somebody deliberately created it; the cached comments behind it still expire on schedule.

What an outside assistant can reach

If you have connected an outside AI assistant (section 8), two of the tools on its fixed list read these cached comments — the list of them, and the ones left by a particular commenter. Both are read-only, and everything in section 8 applies unchanged: what it reads leaves our platform, and from that point it is covered by that operator’s terms and not by this statement. If you would rather an assistant could not see comments, narrow its connection when you make it, or do not connect a channel.

Turning it off

You can stop watching a channel in Attaché at any time, and there is nothing to revoke anywhere else, because we never held a permission to revoke.

Unlike a mailbox, stopping does not erase what was already collected — and we would rather say that than let the paragraph above imply otherwise. The comments already cached stay until they reach their 30 days and the daily job removes them; stopping means only that no new ones arrive. Ask us if you want them gone sooner. Closing your workspace deletes them with everything else, as described in section 11.

Three things outlive the comments: the list of channels somebody chose to watch, our own count of how many reads we made each day, and the receipts recording how many rows each day’s deletion took out. None of the three contains a comment, a commenter’s name, or a commenter’s channel id.

  • To run AI features you invoke, as described in section 7.

  • To keep the platform working — diagnosing errors, capacity planning, and improving the product in aggregate.

We do not sell your personal information, and we do not share it for cross-context behavioral advertising. We do not use your workspace content to train AI models, ours or anyone else's.

Where the GDPR applies to our own controller-side processing, our legal bases are performance of a contract (running your subscription), legitimate interests (securing the platform, preventing abuse, improving the product), and legal obligation (tax and accounting records).

AI processing

All AI features in Attaché run on Google Gemini, called through a single point in our code so that every model call is recorded with its job kind, model tier and token count.

We send only the text a specific task needs — the note you asked it to summarise, the document you asked it to read, the question you typed. Your mail is not one of those tasks. An earlier version of this statement named the case where up to 8,000 characters of a message body went to Gemini for a timeline summary; that path was removed with the mail connector on 09-26-2026, and since no body is fetched at all (section 4) there is none to send.

Training. Google's paid API terms exclude the inputs and outputs of these calls from training Google's models. We do not train models on your data ourselves.

Region. These calls go to a global Google endpoint with no region selection. Google's regional-endpoint product is not in use.

AI output can be wrong. Summaries, extractions and suggestions are drafts for a person to check, and anything read out of an external document is confirmed by a human before it is written into your records.

Outside AI assistants

You can connect an outside AI assistant to your workspace — Claude, or any other product that speaks the Model Context Protocol — so that you can ask it about your records in its own window instead of ours. This is deliberately a separate section from section 7, because it is a different thing in the way that matters here. Section 7 is us sending a short piece of text to a model we chose, under terms we negotiated, and bringing the answer back. This is you handing a company we have no contract with a door into your workspace.

Nothing is connected until somebody on your account connects it. A new workspace has none. Turning one on means a person holding a seat signs in through the assistant and approves the connection on our own screen, the way they would approve any other application asking for access. We cannot connect one for you, and there is no assistant that arrives already switched on.

A connected assistant can never see or do more than the person who connected it. Its permissions are that person's permissions, resolved freshly on every single call rather than copied at the time of connection — so a connection made by somebody who cannot see your invoices cannot see your invoices, and a connection made by somebody whose access is later reduced is reduced with them. The consent screen shows that person the actual list of tools the connection would get, computed the same way.

What a connected assistant can read

It reads through a fixed list of sixteen tools — who am I, search records, read one record, list deals, list invoices, list files, what is on my plate, and the rest. That list lives in our code. The assistant cannot run a query of its own against your database, cannot reach a table no tool exposes, and cannot widen the list by asking.

It cannot read a connected mailbox at all. No tool in that list returns mail, so an assistant cannot reach even the passing digest described in section 4, with the mailbox switched on or off. That is a consequence of the list, not a setting, and if it ever changes this section changes with it.

What it can change — and the desk everything waits at

On its own, nothing. An assistant can name thirty-three actions in your workspace, and they all arrive through one door. Ten of them are things it can actually ask to have done. The other twenty-three it cannot run at all — it is told so and handed the address of the page where a person does it, which is a sentence we would rather it read than a button we would rather not build.

Each of the ten parks as a request on an approvals desk inside Attaché, where a person reads what was asked and either runs it or does not. A parked request expires by itself after 24 hours, and an assistant may hold at most twenty waiting at once, so a connection that starts asking for things cannot bury you in them or leave one lying around for a week.

The same desk stands in front of the two tools that touch files: adding a file to a record, and making a link that opens a file without a login. Neither happens because an assistant asked for it. A person approves the link, and the link itself stops working after 30 days.

One write does not wait, and we would rather name it than bury it. An assistant can tell your workspace a fact to remember — the way you would tell a colleague something. That goes straight into the same agent memory your own agents use, where it is listed on a page in the app with a button to forget it — and forgetting takes it off every one of your agents at once, because one fact is taught to all of them. We record which assistant said it; the page today lists it alongside the things your own agent worked out for itself. It changes no record, no deal and no invoice; it only changes what your agents know.

What is refused outright

Anything carrying a credential never reaches the desk at all. The actions that hold a secret — connecting a phone number, connecting an outside account — are refused entry to the assistant interface entirely, so there is no approval screen on which somebody half-reading it could hand an outside assistant a key to one of your other services. Actions that work by asking a person a question are refused for the same reason: an assistant cannot put words in your staff's mouths.

What is written down

Every call an assistant makes is recorded, including the ones we refuse, on a page in the app you can read. The record says which tool was called and how it ended, plus — in the three cases where naming it tells a reader something — which change was asked for, what a file was called, or which kind of record was opened. It deliberately does not record what came back — not the records read, not the words searched for — because a log of everything an assistant was ever shown would be a second copy of your workspace sitting in a place with weaker rules around it.

A connection can also be narrowed when it is made — to sales, money, people, work, marketing or setup, rather than all of them — so an assistant brought in for one job need not be given the run of the place.

How long access lasts, and how to end it

An assistant's access key is good for one hour; the standing grant behind it for 30 days, after which the person signs in again. Disconnecting an assistant kills both at once — there is no key that outlives the grant that issued it — and anything that assistant left waiting on the approvals desk is still just a request nobody ran. An administrator ends a connection from the agent activity page in Attaché; anyone can also stop one at the assistant's end, which is the faster route if it is your own.

The part we cannot control, said plainly

Everything above is about what happens on our side of the line. Here is the other side. What a connected assistant reads leaves our platform. It goes to whoever operates that assistant, and from that moment it is covered by their privacy terms and their retention, not by this statement and not by anything we can enforce. We do not see what they do with it. If that company trains on what it is sent, or keeps it, that is a question to settle with them before you connect it — and it is worth settling, because the whole point of connecting one is that it can read your customer records.

We do not reach back the other way. An outside assistant usually has connections of its own — its own mail, its own calendar, its own files. Attaché cannot see or use any of them today; nothing in our code asks an assistant for anything. We may want to in future, because an assistant that can read the thread a deal actually lives in would be genuinely useful. If we build it, it will be something you switch on, and it will appear in the table in section 9 before it is switched on, like everything else that gets access to your data.

Short version: an outside assistant is a reader with a narrow list and no hands. The limits in here are ours to keep. What happens to your records after they leave is the assistant operator's, which is why the decision to connect one belongs to you rather than to us.

Where your data is processed

Attaché runs one set of infrastructure for every customer worldwide, located in the United States. We do not create per-region infrastructure, and we do not intend to. A self-serve product priced per seat cannot carry a separate database and storage footprint per jurisdiction; regional infrastructure is what a contracted single-tenant deployment buys, and it is priced accordingly.

Said plainly rather than buried: if you are outside the United States, you are served from infrastructure inside the United States. Personal data of people in the EU and UK is processed in the US — by the platform and by Gemini. Where we rely on a transfer instrument for that, it is the EU-US Data Privacy Framework where a provider is certified, and standard contractual clauses otherwise.

If you are under a hard data-residency obligation — a regulated financial institution, a public body, a controller with a residency clause in its own contracts — Attaché self-serve is probably not the right product for you, and you should not connect a mailbox to it. Talk to us about a contracted deployment instead. We would rather say this here than have you find it out later.

How long we keep it

Your workspace content stays until you delete it or close your account. We do not age it out on a schedule.

Cached YouTube comments are the one exception, and they are not ours to keep. They are deleted 30 days after we fetched them, whether or not the channel is still being watched, because YouTube’s developer policies set that limit. Section 5 explains it in full.

When you close your account, we delete your workspace. Your records live in a database of your own and your files are stored under your workspace's own name, so deleting everything we hold on you is one operation rather than a hunt through shared tables. It removes your records, your files, and your registration with us. We do this within 30 days of closure, and sooner on request. Ask us and we will confirm in writing when it is done.

Backups are the one honest exception. Our automated database backups still hold a copy of your data until they expire on their normal cycle, which is no longer than 30 days after the deletion. They are encrypted, they are not used to serve the product, and nothing is restored from them to bring your data back.

Two things outlive that deletion, and you should know about both. Ordinary backups roll off on their own cycle, so data can persist in a backup for a short period after it is gone from the live system; it is not restored to anyone. And we keep billing and tax records for as long as the law requires us to, independently of your workspace.

Before you close an account, export what you want to keep. Section 13 covers how.

How we protect it

  • Tenant isolation. Each customer gets its own PostgreSQL schema — a separate namespace with its own copy of every table, not a shared table with a customer column on it. The schema is fixed when a database connection is opened and never changed afterward, so a connection serving one customer has never been able to address another's tables. This is the strongest control we have and the reason it is listed first.
  • Passwords are stored using scrypt with a per-user random salt and compared in constant time. They are never reversible and never written to a log.
  • Sessions are a signed, HTTP-only cookie marked secure in production — not a token sitting in browser storage where a script could read it.
  • Multi-factor authentication is available to every user via an authenticator app. It is per user and is not currently something an administrator can force on the whole organization.
  • Encryption. TLS for all traffic between your browser and us and between us and our providers. Platform-managed encryption at rest on the database and on file storage.
  • Authorization and audit. Permissions are named capabilities resolved per role and adjustable per organization. Every write goes through one layer that applies the permission check and records the audit entry, so neither can be skipped by new code.
  • Access by our staff is limited to the people who need it to operate and support the platform.

No system is perfectly secure, and we will not claim otherwise. If you believe you have found a vulnerability, write to support@attache.group and we will respond.

If you are in the EEA, the UK or Switzerland, you may also complain to your local data protection authority. We would rather you came to us first.

Cookies

This website — attache.group — sets no cookies at all. It is static HTML and CSS with no scripts, no analytics, no tag manager, no advertising pixels and no embedded third-party content. Nothing here follows you anywhere.

The application sets two cookies, both strictly necessary and neither used for tracking: a signed session cookie that keeps you signed in, and a short-lived cookie used while completing a multi-factor challenge. Both are HTTP-only. Blocking them means you cannot sign in.

Children

Attaché is a business tool sold to businesses. It is not directed at children, and we do not knowingly collect personal information from anyone under 16. If you believe a child's information has reached us, write to us and we will delete it.
